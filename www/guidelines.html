Overview & Treatment Goals

Asthma is a heterogeneous disease characterized by chronic airway inflammation and variable respiratory symptoms (wheeze, shortness of breath, chest tightness, cough) together with variable expiratory airflow limitation. The GINA 2024 strategy emphasizes personalized, treatable-trait-based management rather than a one-size-fits-all approach.

ðŸ’¡ Core Treatment Goals

To achieve good symptom control (minimize daytime symptoms, night waking, reliever use, activity limitation) and to minimize future risk of exacerbations, fixed airflow limitation, and treatment side-effects.

The cornerstone of modern GINA strategy is the move away from SABA-only treatment. Since 2019, GINA no longer recommends treatment with SABA alone for adults and adolescents due to evidence of increased severe exacerbation risk compared with ICS-containing regimens.

Diagnosis & Classification

Diagnosis requires evidence of variable respiratory symptoms and variable expiratory airflow limitation. Confirm with spirometry (bronchodilator reversibility) or, if unavailable, peak expiratory flow variability.

Acute Exacerbation Management

An exacerbation is an acute or subacute worsening of symptoms and/or a decrease in expiratory airflow from baseline. Prompt recognition and treatment are essential to prevent progression to status asthmaticus.

Inhaler Technique & Adherence

Poor inhaler technique is associated with poor asthma control and increased exacerbation risk. Up to 80% of patients have incorrect technique. Check technique at every opportunity.

  • Choose the inhaler device the patient can use correctly and prefers
  • Demonstrate technique; ask patient to demonstrate back
  • Address physical barriers (arthritis, cognitive impairment)
  • Always prescribe a spacer with MDI where feasible
  • Re-check technique at every clinical encounter and after any change in device
  • Assess adherence non-judgmentally; identify barriers (cost, complexity, beliefs)

Special Populations

Pregnancy: Asthma control must be maintained. ICS (especially budesonide), ICS-LABA, and montelukast have excellent safety data. Uncontrolled asthma poses far greater risk to mother and fetus than controller medications.

Children 6â€“11 years: Step 1 preferred is regular low-dose ICS (not as-needed ICS-formoterol, which is not approved in this age in all regions). Step 2: low-dose ICS. Step 3: low-dose ICS-LABA or medium-dose ICS.

Elderly: Higher risk of ICS side-effects (pneumonia, osteoporosis). Use lowest effective dose. Check drug interactions (e.g., beta-blockers, NSAIDs). Consider physical barriers to inhaler use.

7

Red Flags & Referral Criteria

Refer to specialist care (pulmonology/allergy/immunology) when any of the following are present:

  • Diagnosis uncertain or alternative diagnoses suspected
  • Persistent poor control despite high-dose ICS-LABA (Step 4)
  • â‰¥2 oral corticosteroid bursts in 12 months
  • Any hospitalization or ICU admission for asthma
  • Significant comorbidities (allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis, nasal polyps, COPD overlap)
  • Need for biologic therapy assessment
  • Occupational asthma suspected
