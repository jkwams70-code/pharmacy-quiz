📌 Principles You Applied

  • Beneficence : actively doing good: prevent harm, remove harm, promote wellbeing.
  • Nonmaleficence : doing no harm: never deliberately or carelessly injure the patient.
  • Autonomy : respect for persons: honour competent patients' own choices.
  • Justice : fairness: distribute benefits and burdens equitably, by medical need.

⚖ Important Rules From the Lecture

  • When beneficence and nonmaleficence conflict, nonmaleficence overrules : two good outcomes never justify harming one patient.
  • A competent patient's informed refusal trumps the practitioner's recommendation.
  • Treatment is obligatory when benefit clearly outweighs risk; not indicated when harm outweighs benefit; otherwise optional.
  • Legal signature ≠ moral informed consent : understanding must be confirmed through reflective conversation.
  • In emergencies with no surrogate and no known wishes, consent may be waived.
  • Override autonomy only when the autonomy interest at stake is trivial (weak paternalism) : never for deeply held values.
  • Allocate by medical need : likely benefit, urgency, quality-of-life change, duration. Never by ability to pay or social worth.
  • Practitioners must not ration at the bedside; they must advocate for their patient.

⚠️ Decisions Worth Revisiting

✅ Practical Pharmacy Checklist

  • Counsel every patient : withholding information violates beneficence.
  • Double-check high-risk drugs (digoxin, insulin, anticoagulants) : "take one tablet daily" must never become "four times daily."
  • Never substitute generic products without evidence of bioequivalence.
  • Confirm understanding by asking the patient to explain back : not just nod.
  • Document treatment preferences in the patient's chart.
  • If competence is in doubt, assess understanding, reasoning and risk/benefit grasp : not whether the patient agrees with you.
  • No living will? Follow the surrogate hierarchy: durable power of attorney → spouse → adult children → parents → siblings.
  • Surrogates must make substituted judgment, not decisions in their own interest.
  • Treat equal medical needs equally : use open, due-process rationing, never bedside rationing.

Act 857 Regulatory Toolkit

Case File 05 · Practical checks for registration, licensing, supervision, and lawful supply

📋 The regulatory pause

Before opening, practising, or supplying: confirm the person is registered, the premises and activity are licensed, responsible supervision is real, the product route is lawful, and records can prove what happened.

1. Practitioner registration

  • Confirm status: Do not allow practice unless the pharmacist or support staff member is registered.
  • Check category: Confirm whether permanent, temporary, or provisional registration permits the proposed work.
  • Check renewal: Registration is time-limited. Keep evidence of current renewal and scope.
  • Check qualifications: Foreign-trained practitioners require recognised qualifications, registration evidence, and other prescribed conditions.

2. Premises and superintendent

  • Identify the licence: Confirm the exact premises, activity, and licence category before opening.
  • Supervision: A licensed corporate or institutional pharmacy must operate under a registered superintendent pharmacist where required.
  • Reality check: Compare the supervision plan with opening hours, staff duties, and pharmacist availability.
  • Separate activities: Verify the requirements for wholesale supply, promotional offices, and OTC sellers.

3. Restricted-medicine supply

  • Classify: Identify prescription-only, pharmacy-only, OTC, and other approved categories.
  • Prescription: Check signature, date, prescriber and patient details, dose, quantity, and prior dispensing.
  • Container and label: Use the prescribed container and required particulars.
  • Record and retain: Complete the Restricted Medicines Record Book or approved electronic record and retain fully dispensed prescriptions for the required period.

4. Scope and delegation

  • Authorised supply: Restricted medicines must be handled by an authorised person or entity, subject to statutory exceptions.
  • Technicians and students: Keep activities within permitted scope and under pharmacist supervision.
  • Emergency assistance: Stay within the conditions for first aid or simple ailments and make the required referral.
  • Escalate: Stop and seek direction when the person, activity, or medicine falls outside scope.

5. Inspection readiness

  • Authority: An authorised inspector should produce authenticated authority on demand.
  • Cooperate: Provide information about the premises, activities, workers, registrations, and licences.
  • Preserve evidence: Do not hide, destroy, alter, or remove relevant records or medicines.
  • Inventory: When articles are taken, ensure the inventory is prepared, signed, and copied to the facility.
  • Public danger: Understand seizure and closure powers where imminent danger, health hazard, or unlicensed operation exists.

6. Due process and accountability

  • Suspension: Proposed suspension requires notice and an opportunity to make representations.
  • Cancellation: Cancellation follows specified grounds, due process, and required notice.
  • Appeal: A person dissatisfied with a Board decision may have a statutory appeal route.
  • Conflicts: Board and committee members must disclose interests and withdraw from affected decisions.
  • Offences: Treat false declarations, unregistered practice, unauthorised premises, obstruction, unlawful possession, and unlicensed supply as serious red flags.

Health Professions Regulatory Bodies Act, 2013 (Act 857) · Detailed Study Note

Detailed study note

Act 857 establishes Ghana’s health-professions regulatory bodies. Part 4 governs the Pharmacy Council, pharmacists, pharmaceutical support staff, pharmacy practice, licensing, restricted medicines, inspection, discipline, and offences. Part 6 provides administrative, financial, staffing, governance, and collaboration provisions.

1. Pharmacy Council and public purpose

The Pharmacy Council is a body corporate with perpetual succession. It has a legal identity separate from its members and may acquire, hold, manage, and dispose of property. Its object is to secure, in the public interest, the highest standards in the practice of pharmacy in Ghana.

2. Functions of the Council

3. Governing Board

The Board includes a chairperson who is a registered pharmacist of at least 10 years standing, a Ministry of Health representative, a training-institution representative, an elected registered pharmacist, three other persons including one woman, an Attorney-General representative, and the Registrar. Members are appointed by the President under Article 70 of the Constitution. The Board ensures the proper and effective performance of the Council’s functions.

4. Mandatory registration

A person must not practise as a pharmacist or pharmaceutical support staff unless registered under Part 4. Applications go to the Registrar in the manner determined by the Board, registration is subject to the prescribed fee and period, and additional qualifications may be entered in the register.

Foreign-trained personMeets qualification requirements, has working English, and provides proof of qualification and registration in the country of training or origin.

5. Registration categories

The Registrar keeps the registers and publishes the annual list of registered practitioners in the Gazette by 31 January. Names may be removed on Board recommendation for death, professional misconduct, or non-payment of fees, and may be restored except where the person is deceased.

6. Suspension, cancellation, and appeal

The Board may suspend registration where an offence or misconduct is being investigated, a false declaration was made, or Part 4 was contravened. Suspension requires at least 30 days’ notice of intention and an opportunity to make representations.

Cancellation follows a Disciplinary Committee recommendation where the practitioner is convicted under Part 4 or its Regulations, loses the qualification supporting registration, is imprisoned for a criminal offence, or fails to comply with a Council penalty after due process. The Board must give at least 30 days’ notice of intention to cancel. A dissatisfied person may appeal to the High Court within 30 days after the decision is communicated.

7. Licences for corporate bodies and OTC sellers

The Board may revoke a licence when Part 4 is contravened or a licence condition ceases to be satisfied.

8. Restricted medicines

Restricted medicines include prescription-only medicines (POM), pharmacy-only medicines (P), OTC medicines, and any other classification approved by the Minister responsible for Health. The Minister classifies medicines and conditions for supply on the advice of the FDA and Pharmacy Council through executive instrument.

9. Prescription supply and records

Where a restricted medicine is supplied under a valid prescription, the supplier enters the date of supply and the supplier’s name and address on the prescription in indelible writing. When fully dispensed, the prescription is retained on the premises for two years and kept readily available for inspection.

10. Valid prescription

A valid prescription must be written in indelible writing, signed and dated by the prescriber, and identify the prescriber and patient. It should state the medicine, strength or quantity where required, dose, directions, and relevant particulars. A prescription must not be treated as a fresh authority when it has already been fully dispensed. When doubt exists, verify before supply.

11. Preparation and supply: Rule 100

Only a pharmacist or a licensed pharmaceutical company may mix, compound, prepare, or supply restricted medicines, subject to the statutory exemptions. Pharmacy technicians, students, and other support staff may act within their authorised scope and under the required supervision. The rule protects patients from unqualified or unsupervised preparation and supply.

12. Exemptions and supply without prescription

Exemptions may cover limited professional or emergency circumstances recognised by the Act or Regulations. They do not create a general permission to bypass prescription controls. Supply without prescription must fit a lawful exception, be clinically and professionally justified, and be documented where required.

13. Medical aid and referral

A pharmacist or support staff member may give first aid after an accident or first-aid treatment for a simple common ailment where it is not reasonably practicable to consult a medical practitioner or dentist. In an emergency, the patient must be referred immediately or within 24 hours after the initial dosage, and the referral should state the medicines used and the extent of treatment given.

14. Entry and investigation

An authorised person may enter premises at a reasonable time to inspect registration or where there is reasonable cause to believe an offence under Part 4 has been or is being committed.

15. Closure of premises

An inspector may close premises selling or supplying restricted medicines where a health hazard may exist or the premises is unlicensed. Closure is carried out with police assistance. If police assistance is not possible, the closure must be reported to the police within 24 hours. A health-hazard order may include conditions determined by the Board.

16. Change of name or address

A registered practitioner must notify the Registrar of a change in name or address within 30 days of the change. Current registration information supports accountability, notices, and public verification.

17. Offences and penalties

General Part 4 offences may attract a fine of 250 to 5,000 penalty units, imprisonment of up to 10 years, or both. A continuing offence may attract an additional 10 penalty units per day after written notice. Directors, secretaries, officers, or partners may be treated as committing an offence, subject to the statutory defence that they lacked knowledge or consent and took reasonable preventive steps.

18. Regulations under Part 4

The Minister may, on the Board’s advice, make Regulations covering registration conditions, practice standards, discipline, fees, premises and licences, public-institution practice, professional-service fees, scope of practice, medicine ranges for health practitioners, and other matters needed to implement Part 4.

19. Part 6: Registrar and administration

The President appoints a pharmacist with at least 10 years of professional experience and at least five years of administrative or managerial experience as Registrar under Article 195. The Registrar serves a four-year term and is eligible for one further term, acts as secretary to the Board, and is also a Board member under the stated appointment provisions.

The Registrar manages day-to-day Council administration and may delegate functions to an officer, but delegation does not remove ultimate responsibility.

21. Funds, staff, and collaboration

Funds may come from Parliamentary appropriations, fees, donations, grants, gifts, and other money approved by the Minister responsible for Finance. A percentage of internally generated funds may be retained as specified in writing. The President appoints staff under Article 195; public officers may be transferred or seconded, and advisers may be engaged on Board recommendation.

The Minister may issue policy directives to the Board, which must comply. The Council advises the Minister and collaborates with other statutory health bodies, particularly the body responsible for facility and premises licensing and inspection. A non-citizen who practises under the Act must have a valid work permit or another lawful entitlement to work.

22. Key takeaways

  • The Pharmacy Council is a statutory body corporate whose object is the highest standards in pharmacy practice in the public interest.
  • Registration is mandatory for pharmacists and pharmaceutical support staff.
  • Permanent, temporary, and provisional registers serve different practice situations.
  • Temporary registration is limited to approved facilities and is subject to significant penalties if breached.
  • Suspension requires 30 days’ notice and an opportunity to make representations; cancellation requires the prescribed disciplinary route and notice.
  • Appeals from a Board decision go to the High Court within 30 days.
  • Corporate bodies, OTC sellers, and wholesale suppliers require the appropriate licences.
  • Restricted medicines include POM, pharmacy-only, and OTC categories.
  • Valid prescriptions, retained records, supervision, and lawful premises protect the medicine supply chain.
  • Inspectors may enter, inspect, require information, take or seize medicines, inventory material, and close hazardous or unlicensed premises.
  • Part 4 offences can carry fines up to 5,000 penalty units or imprisonment up to 10 years.
  • The Registrar, Board, committees, Minister, and other statutory bodies each have defined administrative roles.

Disciplinary Procedure Toolkit

Use this before responding to, reporting, or managing a professional-discipline matter.

1. Separate allegation from finding

Start with the allegation: Record exactly what is said, who is involved, what medicine or service is concerned, and what evidence exists. Do not announce guilt: A complaint triggers a process; it does not replace an enquiry.

2. Screen the professional-risk issue

  • Was a restricted, Class A, or Class B medicine dispensed by an unqualified person?
  • Was medicine supplied while a pharmacist was intoxicated or impaired?
  • Was an unwholesome medicine dispensed?
  • Is there evidence of fraud, dishonesty, collusion, false information, or misuse of registration?
  • Does the conduct suggest a risk to patients, records, or public confidence?

3. Preserve the evidence

Secure prescriptions, dispensing records, stock and batch information, registration documents, duty rosters, payment records, messages, inspection notes, witness names, and the original complaint. Keep the date, source, and chain of custody clear.

4. Use the correct procedural route

  1. Complaint or information reaches the Registrar.
  2. Registrar refers the matter and serves the pharmacist within the required period.
  3. Pharmacist is invited to submit a written explanation within 30 days from receipt.
  4. Committee examines the complaint and explanation to decide whether an enquiry is necessary.
  5. Persons and documents may be summoned where they can assist the enquiry.
  6. Enquiry is conducted formally, with oath, examination, representation, and a recorded proceeding.
  7. Committee sends its record and recommendation to the Board.
  8. Board accepts, remits, or modifies the recommendation and imposes an appropriate penalty where justified.

5. Responding as the pharmacist

Check the date of service, identify each allegation, preserve relevant records, answer factually, distinguish what you know from what you dispute, identify witnesses and documents, and obtain appropriate legal or expert support. Never ignore the notice without a reasonable basis.

6. Communicating a penalty

Where cancellation or suspension is ordered, track the notice to deliver the certificate, Gazette publication, deletion from the register where applicable, and any appeal period. Do not treat an appeal as irrelevant: the regulations restrict action until the appeal period expires or the appeal is disposed of, as applicable.

7. Re-registration check

After cancellation, re-registration requires an application supported by declarations of good behaviour from a senior practising pharmacist with at least 15 years of practice and a senior public officer with at least 10 years of public service.

Pharmacy Practice (Disciplinary Procedure and Fees) Regulations, 1998 (L.I. 1645) · Detailed Study Note

Detailed study note

L.I. 1645 establishes the procedure for disciplining registered pharmacists in Ghana. It protects the public from professional misconduct while requiring complaint referral, notice, explanation, evidence, a formal enquiry, Board decision-making, and lawful implementation of penalties.

1. Overview and governing principle

The Pharmacy Practice (Disciplinary Procedure and Fees) Regulations, 1998 (L.I. 1645) governs disciplinary action against registered pharmacists, the investigation and adjudication of professional misconduct, penalties imposed by the Board, and re-registration after cancellation.

No disciplinary action without enquiry: disciplinary action cannot be taken against a registered pharmacist unless it results from an enquiry held in accordance with the Regulations. A complaint is a trigger for process, not a final finding of guilt.

2. Complaint and referral

Where a written complaint or information is received by the Registrar, or the Registrar otherwise becomes aware that a registered pharmacist may be involved in a relevant matter, the Registrar submits the complaint, information, or a written statement to the Disciplinary Committee.

3. Meaning of professional misconduct

Professional misconduct is behaviour, action, or pronouncement outside the bounds of what is acceptable or worthy of membership by the governing body of the profession. It includes conduct that threatens patient safety, honesty, lawful practice, professional trust, or the standing of pharmacy.

5. Service of complaint and written explanation

Within seven days of reference to the Disciplinary Committee, the Registrar serves the pharmacist with a copy of the complaint or information. The pharmacist is invited to submit a written explanation to the Committee within 30 days from the date of receipt.

ScreeningCommittee examines the complaint and explanation to determine whether enquiry is necessary.

6. Summoning persons and documents

After considering the complaint and explanation, the Committee may summon the pharmacist, complainant, and other persons likely to assist. A summoned person may be required to produce documents or other material in their possession or control that relates to the enquiry. The power is limited: a person cannot be compelled to produce a document that could not be compelled in court.

7. The formal enquiry

Cross-examinationAnother person appearing may examine or cross-examine as permitted.
RepresentationThe pharmacist or other required person may appear with a lawyer or expert.
AbsenceIf an explanation was submitted but the pharmacist fails to appear without reasonable cause, the enquiry may proceed in their absence.

These safeguards support both public accountability and a fair opportunity to answer. Attendance is important, but unjustified absence does not automatically establish guilt and does not necessarily stop the process.

8. Conclusion of enquiry

At the conclusion, the Committee submits the record of the enquiry to the Board together with its recommendation on penalty, where the recommendation has majority support. Matters before the Committee are decided by simple majority.

9. Failure to submit an explanation

If the pharmacist fails without reasonable cause to provide the explanation within the specified period, the Registrar must first satisfy himself that the notice was served. The information is then brought before the Committee, which considers the complaint and makes recommendations to the Board. Silence therefore does not erase the allegation or prevent the process from continuing.

12. Suspension or cancellation procedure

Where registration is suspended or cancelled, the Registrar serves notice informing the pharmacist of the order and requiring delivery of the certificate within 30 days of service. Notice of the suspension or cancellation is published in the Gazette. For cancellation, the pharmacist’s name is deleted from the Register of Pharmacists.

If the pharmacist appeals, the Registrar acts under the relevant provision only after the notice-of-appeal period expires or after the appeal is disposed of. At the end of a suspension, the certificate is returned and notice of the expiry is published in the Gazette.

13. Re-registration after cancellation

Re-registration after cancellation requires an application supported by declarations of good behaviour from two persons:

  • A senior practising pharmacist with not less than 15 years of practice.
  • A senior public officer of a public service with not less than 10 years of service.

Cancellation therefore has a lasting professional effect, but the Regulations provide a defined route through which a future application may be considered.

FDA Product-Safety Toolkit

Use this before accepting, promoting, storing, supplying, or disposing of a regulated product.

1. Identify the regulated article

Classify the item as a drug, herbal medicinal product, cosmetic, medical device, or household chemical substance. Record the product name, manufacturer, source, batch, expiry, packaging, and intended use.

2. Verify registration and premises

  • Check the FDA registration number and conditions.
  • Confirm the supplier and import or distribution documents.
  • Confirm that the premises is registered for the activity.
  • Check the relevant manufacturing, storage, importer, exporter, or other licence.
  • Do not treat a supplier assurance or pending application as registration.

3. Screen the label and claim

Compare the label and advertisement with the registered product and approved conditions. Stop claims that are false, misleading, deceptive, misbranded, or likely to make one article mistaken for another. Public treatment, prevention, or cure advertising requires FDA approval and some diseases are specifically restricted.

4. Check the shipment

  • Confirm the product is registered and the import route is authorised.
  • For finished imported products, look for the required certificate of analysis or certificate of pharmaceutical product where applicable.
  • Check remaining shelf life, especially the statutory thresholds for imported drugs and herbal products.
  • Look for signs of counterfeit identity, source, substitution, imitation, or false manufacturer marks.

5. Quarantine and preserve

Separate questionable, damaged, counterfeit-suspect, unregistered, or contaminated products. Do not sell, return, relabel, or destroy them casually. Record quantities, location, batch, condition, photographs, and who placed the stock on hold.

6. Escalate correctly

Notify the responsible pharmacist and use the FDA reporting or inspection route where public risk or suspected non-compliance exists. Cooperate with lawful sampling and laboratory analysis. Keep the chain of custody clear.

7. Safe disposal

Unwholesome regulated products should be disposed of under FDA supervision. A pharmacy should not privately destroy or discard affected stock in a way that could expose people or allow the products to re-enter circulation.

Detailed study note

Public Health Act, 2012 (Act 851), Parts 7, 8 and 9. This note summarizes Ghana's FDA framework for product standards, registration, licensing, public protection, and enforcement.

1. Overview of Act 851

The Public Health Act, 2012 (Act 851) is the principal legislation governing public health in Ghana. Parts 7, 8 & 9 specifically deal with:

  • Food and Drugs — establishing the Food and Drugs Authority (FDA) and regulating food, drugs, cosmetics, medical devices, household chemical substances, and herbal medicinal products
  • Standards — setting and enforcing standards for regulated products
  • Enforcement — inspection, registration, licensing, and penalties
Key Point: Act 851 (Parts 7, 8 & 9) establishes the FDA and provides the legal framework for regulating drugs and related products in Ghana. It works alongside Act 857 (health professions) and Act 829 (health facilities).

2. Legal Structure: The Three Regulatory Bodies

Regulatory Body Established Under Responsibilities
Food and Drugs Authority (FDA) Public Health Act 2012 (Act 851) Drug Control, Manufacturing Premises
Pharmacy Council (PC) Health Professions Regulatory Bodies Act 2013 (Act 857) Practitioners, Pharmaceutical Companies, Practice Standards
Health Facilities Regulatory Agency (HFRA) Health Institutions and Facilities Act 2011 (Act 829) Health Facilities
Key Point: Three separate bodies regulate different aspects of the health sector: FDA (products), PC (practitioners), and HFRA (facilities).

3. Establishment of the Food and Drugs Authority

(1) There is hereby established a body corporate to be known as the Food and Drugs Authority.
(2) Where there is hindrance to the acquisition of property, the property may be acquired for the Authority under the State Property and Contracts Act, 1960 (CA. 6) or the State Lands Act, 1962 (Act 125) , and the costs shall be borne by the Authority.
Key Point: The FDA is a body corporate with perpetual succession, capable of acquiring and holding property.

4. Object of the Authority

The object of the Authority is to provide and enforce standards for the sale of:
# Product
1 Food
2 Herbal medicinal products
3 Cosmetics
4 Drugs
5 Medical devices
6 Household chemical substances
Key Point: The FDA's mandate covers six categories of regulated products: food, herbal medicines, cosmetics, drugs, medical devices, and household chemicals.

5. Functions of the Authority

To achieve its object, the Authority shall:

# Function
(a) Ensure adequate and effective standards for food, drugs, cosmetics, household chemicals, and medical devices
(b) Monitor through the District Assemblies and any other agency of State compliance with the provisions of this Part
(c) Advise the Minister on measures for the protection of the health of consumers
(d) Advise the Minister on the preparation of effective Regulations for the implementation of this Part
(e) Approve the initiation and conduct of clinical trials in the country
(f) Perform any other functions that are ancillary to attaining the objects of the Authority
Key Point: The FDA's functions include standard-setting, monitoring, advising the Minister, approving clinical trials, and any ancillary functions.

14. Prohibited Sale of Regulated Products

A person commits an offence if that person sells a drug, herbal medicinal product, cosmetic, medical device, or household chemical substance which:
# Prohibition
(a) Has in or on it a substance that may cause injury to the health of the user when the article is used: (i) according to the directions on the label; or (ii) for a purpose and by a method of use that is customary or usual
(b) Consists in whole or in part of a filthy, rotten, decomposed, or diseased substance or of a foreign matter likely to cause injury
(c) Is adulterated
(d) Is prepared, preserved, packed, or stored under insanitary conditions
Key Point: Selling harmful, contaminated, adulterated, or insanitary products is a criminal offence.

15. Public Protection Against Harm or Injury

The law protects the public against products that:

# Ground

2 Consist in whole or part of filthy, rotten, decomposed, or diseased substances or foreign matter
3 Are adulterated
4 Are prepared, preserved, packed, or stored under insanitary conditions
Key Point: Public protection is the overarching goal — products must be safe, wholesome, unadulterated, and hygienically prepared.

16. Standards for Regulated Products

A. When a Standard Is Prescribed

Where a standard is prescribed for a product, a person who manufactures, labels, packages, sells, or advertises any other substance in a manner likely to be mistaken for that product commits an offence — unless the substance is the product in question and complies with the prescribed standard.

B. When a Standard Is Not Prescribed but Published

Where a standard has not been prescribed but is contained in a publication specified in the Fourth Schedule, a person who labels, packages, sells, or advertises any other substance likely to be mistaken for that product commits an offence.

C. When a Standard Is Neither Prescribed nor Published

A person who manufactures, labels, packages, sells, or advertises a product for which no standard has been prescribed or published commits an offence unless the product:
- (a) Is in accordance with the professed standard under which it is labelled, sold, or advertised; and
- (b) Does not resemble a product for which a standard has been prescribed or published
Key Point: Products must comply with prescribed standards. If no standard exists, the product must match its professed standard and not resemble a standardized product.

When a Product Is Misbranded

(c) Its label, container, or anything accompanying it bears a statement, design, or device which makes a false claim for the product, or which is false or misleading
Key Point: Misbranding includes false, misleading, or deceptive labeling, packaging, or advertising. Products cannot claim greater value than they possess.

📋 The decision rule

Pause before supply. A busy queue, a familiar supplier, or a patient’s urgent need does not replace the legal and professional checks required before medicines enter stock or reach a patient.

1. Verify the product

  • Identity: Confirm the medicine name, strength, dosage form, batch number, expiry date, quantity, and packaging.
  • Registration: Check that the product is authorised for the Ghanaian market and that its markings and documentation are credible.
  • Condition: Inspect seals, storage conditions, temperature records where relevant, and signs of tampering or damage.
  • Mismatch: Stop the receipt when the invoice, delivery note, pack, or batch details do not agree.

2. Verify the supply chain

  • Supplier: Confirm the supplier’s identity, authorisation, contact details, and right to supply the product.
  • Premises: Medicines must be received, stored, and supplied through premises authorised for the activity.
  • Records: Keep purchase documents, invoices, batch information, stock movements, returns, and disposal records.
  • Traceability: A product should be traceable from the supplier to the patient and back again.

3. Respond to a red flag

  1. Quarantine: Separate the questionable stock and mark it clearly as not for sale or supply.
  2. Escalate: Inform the responsible pharmacist or superintendent and follow the pharmacy’s SOP.
  3. Document: Record what was found, when it was found, who supplied it, and what action was taken.
  4. Report: Use the appropriate regulatory and organisational reporting route when safety, quality, or legality is in doubt.
  5. Protect patients: Do not allow commercial pressure or a waiting queue to turn an unresolved concern into a dispensing decision.

Pharmacy Law and Regulation · Detailed Study Note

Detailed study note

Pharmacy law and regulation protect the public where medicines, information, professional judgment, and commercial pressure meet. This note explains why drugs require special controls, how Ghana’s regulatory bodies divide responsibility, the tools they use, and the pharmacist’s role in making the system work.

1. Philosophical basis for pharmacy laws

Drugs are not ordinary commodities. The patient often does not choose the medicine directly, may lack the information needed to compare products, and may suffer serious or fatal harm from a wrong choice. Normal market assumptions about perfect information and rational choice therefore do not work well for medicines. Government intervention is justified by the information gap, the hazardous consequences of error, and the need to protect patients.

2. What is law?

Society has many rules, but the distinguishing feature of law is enforceability. Pharmacy law converts public policy and professional expectations into enforceable duties, permissions, restrictions, and remedies.

3. Functions of law

Law promotes order, stability, and cohesion by formalising public policy through an enactment that can be administered and enforced.

4. Historical pharmacy legislation in Ghana

Ghanaian pharmacy regulation developed from pre-independence instruments such as the Pharmacy and Poisons Ordinance, Dangerous Drugs Ordinance, and Undesirable Advertisement Ordinance. Post-independence laws included the Pharmacy and Drug Act, 1961 (Act 64), related regulations and amendments, the Narcotic Drugs (Control, Enforcement and Sanctions) Law, 1990, the Food and Drugs Law, 1992 (PNDCL 305B), and the Pharmacy Practice (Disciplinary Procedure and Fees) Regulations, 1998 (L.I. 1645).

Earlier provisions also recognised oversight of Pharmacy Board decisions. The supplied notes identify review and appeal routes involving the Commissioner responsible for Health, including approval, rescission, variation, and review of decisions in the specified circumstances. Always confirm the current governing law before relying on an historical provision.

5. What is regulation?

Regulation means controlling conduct through rules or principles. The rules describe desired behaviour, while regulatory administration oversees conformity. Regulation gives government a way to formalise and institutionalise its commitment to protect consumers, providers, investors, and the integrity of the health system.

7. Why pharmacy regulation is necessary

Pharmacy involves different products, professions, and practices, each requiring an appropriate regulatory approach. Consumers may be unable to compare medicine prices, assess product quality, or evaluate the quality of a professional service. Regulation therefore compensates for information asymmetry and protects people who cannot safely judge a medicine by appearance or marketing alone.

Functions are spread across agencies. This allows specialised oversight but creates a need for information sharing and coordinated action. A product problem may require FDA action, a practitioner issue may require Pharmacy Council action, and a premises issue may require HFRA action. The pharmacist should identify the relevant authority rather than assume that one body controls every part of the problem.

The existence of a law does not guarantee compliance. Implementation, inspection, coordination, enforcement, and public awareness determine whether legal protection is experienced in practice.

17. Overcoming key challenges

  • Strengthen FDA powers and capacity to prosecute where authorised.
  • Balance pre-registration controls with post-registration monitoring.
  • Expand pharmacovigilance and post-market surveillance.
  • Require and support continuing education and professional development.
  • Register and regulate all categories of relevant practitioners.
  • Improve regulation of training institutions and standards.
  • Strengthen clinical-trial oversight and technical advisory systems.
  • Improve coordination among FDA, Pharmacy Council, HFRA, District Assemblies, facilities, and professionals.

The pharmacist must protect patient wellbeing and safety, practise ethically and professionally, remain competent, and promote ethical principles. This role includes refusing unsafe or unlawful supply, checking the source and quality of medicines, maintaining records, supervising delegated work, reporting concerns, and helping patients understand safe medicine use.

  • Cooperate: Work honestly with enforcement and regulatory authorities.
  • Expose wrongdoing: Do not participate in illegal or unethical conduct because a colleague, owner, or supplier requests it.

3. Promotion and public trust

  • Use approved claims: Promotion must remain consistent with the product’s registration and package information.
  • Avoid pressure: Do not use sales methods designed to make the public obtain more medicine than reasonably required.
  • Protect professional identity: Do not allow another person to use your name, qualifications, address, or photograph to distribute a medicinal product.

4. Confidentiality and client participation

  • Invite participation: Encourage clients to take part in decisions and respect their preferences.
  • Protect information: Keep patient and family information confidential.
  • Disclose carefully: Share information only with consent, appropriate authority, or where the patient’s interest justifies disclosure.
  • Counsel: Give enough information and advice for safe and effective medicine use.

5. Delegation and accountability

  • Check competence: Assign relevant tasks only to people who are competent for them.
  • Supervise: A delegated task does not transfer the pharmacist’s professional accountability.
  • Follow SOPs: Make sure procedures and policies are available, understood, and followed.
  • Own the decision: A pharmacist accepting responsibility for a pharmacy function remains accountable for supply and care decisions.

Case File 04 · Practical checks from the Patients Charter

📋 The patient-first pause

Before you move the queue forward, ask: Does the patient understand enough to decide? Is the conversation private enough? Have I explained the cost and next steps? Am I treating the patient fairly and safely?

1. Access, dignity, and non-discrimination

  • Offer fair care: Do not reduce the quality of service because of age, disability, gender, language, religion, culture, location, illness, or economic difficulty.
  • Adapt the service: Use understandable language and reasonable support for disability, hearing, literacy, or communication needs.
  • Protect dignity: Avoid discussing a patient’s condition where unnecessary listeners can hear.

2. Information and shared decisions

  • Explain the medicine: Cover purpose, directions, important risks, expected effects, and what to do when problems arise.
  • Check understanding: Invite questions and ask the patient to explain the plan in their own words where appropriate.
  • Respect choice: A patient may ask about alternatives, another provider, or a second opinion. Do not treat questions as disobedience.
  • Recognise emergencies: In an urgent situation where the patient cannot decide, the immediate need for treatment may change the normal information sequence.

3. Privacy, confidentiality, and observers

  • Create a private moment: Move sensitive counselling away from the public queue when possible.
  • Identify everyone: Tell the patient who is providing care and who is present, including students, trainees, and ancillary workers.
  • Limit disclosure: Do not share information with relatives, friends, employers, or other third parties without the patient’s consent or another valid basis such as law or public interest.
  • Teaching use: Seek consent when patient notes or cases are used for teaching, conferences, or similar purposes.

4. Money, policies, and complaints

  • Explain charges: Tell the patient the price, payment method, and anticipated expenditure before treatment where possible.
  • Make exemptions visible: Do not assume a patient knows that an exemption or support facility exists.
  • Explain the route: Patients and recognised representatives should know how to raise a complaint, dispute, or conflict.
  • Keep the response respectful: A complaint is not permission to withhold care or expose the patient’s information.

5. Patient responsibilities and professional support

  • Invite accurate history: Ask for the information needed for safe counselling and explain why it matters.
  • Support adherence: Encourage the patient to follow the prescribed plan, report adverse effects, attend follow-up, and disclose anticipated barriers.
  • Make early help easier: Explain when the patient should return or seek professional help instead of waiting for deterioration.
  • Respect representatives: For minors or people unable to make informed decisions, work with an accredited or recognised representative while preserving the patient’s dignity and interests.

Record the information that matters to continuity and safety: the counselling given, the patient’s questions or refusal, relevant consent, referrals, complaints route explained, and any escalation. Document facts without unnecessary exposure of private details.
